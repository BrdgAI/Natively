# Section 05 — Pronunciation & Speaking Guide

> Goal: Sound confident and fluent when speaking technical terms out loud — especially Big-O notation, algorithm names, numbers in constraints, and common technical phrases. This section is designed to be practiced OUT LOUD, not just read.

---

## How to Use This Section

**Rule: You must say every item in this section out loud at least 3 times.**

Reading it silently is not enough. Speaking technical terms aloud is a completely different skill from reading them. The interview requires speaking. Practice speaking.

**Daily practice:** 5 minutes of speaking the phrases in Part 5 (Spoken Drills) out loud.

---

## PART 1 — Big-O Notation: How to Say It

This is critical. Many people who learned programming from text can write O(n log n) but have never said it aloud. These are the exact sounds to make:

| Written | Say this | Common mistake |
|---------|---------|---------------|
| O(1) | **"Oh of one"** | Saying "O-one" without the "of" |
| O(n) | **"Oh of en"** | Saying "O-n" (skipping "of") |
| O(log n) | **"Oh of log en"** | Saying "Oh-log-en" (skip nothing, include "of") |
| O(n log n) | **"Oh of en log en"** | Rushing it: "Oh-en-log-en" — each word needs space |
| O(n²) | **"Oh of en squared"** | Saying "Oh of en two" — it's "squared" not "two" |
| O(n³) | **"Oh of en cubed"** | Saying "en three" — it's "cubed" not "three" |
| O(2^n) | **"Oh of two to the en"** | Saying "two-n" — it's "two to the n" |
| O(n!) | **"Oh of en factorial"** | Not knowing how to say "!" — it's "factorial" |
| O(V + E) | **"Oh of V plus E"** | Saying "Oh of vee-ee" |
| O(V × E) | **"Oh of V times E"** | Saying "Oh of vee-ee" (ambiguous) |
| O((V + E) log V) | **"Oh of V plus E, times log V"** | Saying it too fast — pause between V+E and log V |
| O(m + n) | **"Oh of em plus en"** | Common for problems with two inputs |
| O(n log k) | **"Oh of en log kay"** | Common for heap-based top-K |
| O(√n) | **"Oh of root en"** or **"Oh of square root of en"** | Saying "root-n" without "of" |

### Practice Saying These Sentences Out Loud:

1. *"The time complexity is O of en log en — the sort is the bottleneck."*
2. *"This is O of V plus E for the graph traversal."*
3. *"Dijkstra's runs in O of V plus E, times log V."*
4. *"The naive approach is O of en squared — too slow for N equals ten to the fifth."*
5. *"I can bring this from O of en squared down to O of en log en by sorting first."*
6. *"Space complexity is O of en for the hash map, O of one for the two pointers."*

## PART 2 — Numbers in Constraints: How to Say Them

When constraints appear in a problem, you need to read them out loud naturally:

| Written | Say this |
|---------|---------|
| 10^4 | **"ten to the fourth"** |
| 10^5 | **"ten to the fifth"** |
| 10^6 | **"ten to the sixth"** |
| 10^9 | **"ten to the ninth"** |
| 10^18 | **"ten to the eighteenth"** |
| -10^4 | **"negative ten to the fourth"** |
| N ≤ 10^5 | **"N is at most ten to the fifth"** or **"N up to ten to the fifth"** |
| 1 ≤ N ≤ 10^4 | **"N is between one and ten to the fourth"** |
| 0 ≤ arr[i] ≤ 10^4 | **"values are between zero and ten to the fourth"** |
| 2^32 | **"two to the thirty-second"** |
| 32-bit integer | **"thirty-two bit integer"** (max value ~2.1 billion) |

### Constraint Reading Practice:

Say each of these out loud:

1. *"N is at most ten to the fifth — so we need at least O of en log en."*
2. *"Values can range from negative ten to the fourth to positive ten to the fourth."*
3. *"With N equals ten to the ninth, we definitely need O of log en or O of one."*
4. *"K is between one and N — so K can be as large as ten to the fifth."*

## PART 3 — Algorithm Names: Correct Pronunciation

| Name | Pronunciation | Notes |
|------|--------------|-------|
| **Dijkstra's** | **DYKE-struh** (rhymes with "bike-struh") | Common error: "die-JEE-stra" — incorrect. The "j" is a "y" sound in Dutch. |
| **Trie** | **TRY** (rhymes with "try" or "dry") | Very commonly mispronounced as "tree" — it is NOT "tree." Say "TRY." |
| **BFS** | **"Bee-Eff-Ess"** (spell it out) | Never say "biffs" |
| **DFS** | **"Dee-Eff-Ess"** (spell it out) | Never say "diffs" |
| **DAG** | **"DAG"** (rhymes with "bag") | Say it as one word, not spelled out |
| **LCA** | **"El-See-Ay"** (spell it out) | Lowest Common Ancestor |
| **API** | **"Ay-Pee-Eye"** | Never "AH-pee" |
| **SQL** | **"ESS-cue-el"** | Some say "sequel" — both are fine, be consistent |
| **Kosaraju** | **"Ko-sa-RAH-joo"** | Rare at L3 but good to know |
| **Bellman-Ford** | **"BELL-man-FORD"** | Two clear words, equal stress |
| **Floyd-Warshall** | **"FLOYD-WAR-shul"** | "Warshall" not "War-shall" |
| **lru_cache** | **"L-R-U cache"** (ell-ar-you) | LRU = Least Recently Used |
| **heapq** | **"heap-queue"** | The Python module name |
| **deque** | **"DEK"** (rhymes with "deck") | From "double-ended queue" — do NOT say "dee-cue" |
| **defaultdict** | **"default-dict"** | Two words as one |
| **Memoization** | **"meh-mo-eye-ZAY-shun"** | Common mistake: "mem-oh-EE-zation" — the stress is on "ZAY" |
| **Amortized** | **"AM-or-tized"** | Stress on "AM" |
| **Heuristic** | **"hyoo-RIS-tic"** | Common mistake: "hoo-RIS-tic" |
| **Recurrence** | **"re-CUR-rence"** | Two R sounds — re-CUR-rence |
| **Adjacency** | **"a-JAY-sen-see"** | Stress on "JAY" |
| **Modular** | **"MOD-yoo-ler"** | |
| **Sentinel** | **"SEN-ti-nel"** | As in "sentinel node" |

## PART 4 — Technical Tongue Twister Drills

These are combinations of technical words that are easy to say wrong when nervous. Practice them slowly first, then at normal speed.

### Drill 1 — Dijkstra's Complexity
Say this 3 times at increasing speed:

*"Dijkstra's algorithm runs in O of V plus E, times log V — using a min-heap with early termination at the destination."*

---

### Drill 2 — BFS vs DFS Explanation
*"BFS explores breadth-first — it visits all neighbors before going deeper. DFS explores depth-first — it goes as deep as possible before backtracking."*

---

### Drill 3 — Relaxation Step
*"The relaxation step updates the shortest known distance — if the new distance through the current node is less than the previously known distance to the neighbor, we update it."*

---

### Drill 4 — Constraint Reading
*"N is at most ten to the fifth, and values range from negative ten to the fourth to positive ten to the fourth."*

---

### Drill 5 — Complexity Chain
*"The brute force is O of en squared — too slow for N equals ten to the fifth. Using a hash map brings it to O of en. Sorting first would give O of en log en."*

---

### Drill 6 — DP Explanation
*"The recurrence relation is: dp of i equals dp of i minus one plus dp of i minus two. The base cases are dp of zero equals one and dp of one equals one."*

---

### Drill 7 — Topological Sort
*"Kahn's algorithm tracks in-degrees. We start with all nodes whose in-degree is zero, process them, and decrement the in-degree of their neighbors. If any neighbor reaches zero in-degree, we enqueue it."*

## PART 5 — Spoken Sentence Starters (Practice These Daily)

These are the most common sentence starters in an interview. They need to come out naturally without thinking. Practice them out loud until they feel automatic.

### Opening a thought:
- *"Let me think through this for a moment..."*
- *"My first instinct here is..."*
- *"The key observation is..."*
- *"This looks like a [pattern] problem to me..."*
- *"Before I start coding, let me make sure I understand the problem..."*

### Introducing brute force:
- *"The naive approach would be to [X] — that gives us O of en squared..."*
- *"A brute force solution would be [X], but that's too slow for N equals ten to the fifth..."*
- *"We could check every possible [X], but that's O of two to the en..."*

### Introducing optimization:
- *"I think we can do better — if I use a hash map here..."*
- *"The sorted property tells me I can use binary search instead..."*
- *"Trading O of en space gives us O of one time per lookup..."*

### Explaining a data structure choice:
- *"I'm using a min-heap here because I need to extract the smallest element repeatedly in O of log en..."*
- *"A hash set gives me O of one membership checks..."*
- *"I'm using a deque instead of a list because popleft is O of one on a deque versus O of en on a list..."*

### Announcing complexity:
- *"Time complexity here is O of en log en — the sort is the bottleneck."*
- *"Space is O of en for the hash map, O of one extra for the pointers."*
- *"This runs in O of V plus E times log V overall."*

### Asking for alignment:
- *"Does this direction seem reasonable to you before I start implementing?"*
- *"Should I go ahead and start coding, or do you want to discuss the approach further?"*
- *"I want to make sure we're aligned before I write code — does this make sense?"*

## PART 6 — Filler Words: What to AVOID and What to USE Instead

### Filler sounds to eliminate:
- **"Um..."** → Replace with a brief pause, or say: *"Let me think..."*
- **"Uh..."** → Replace with: *"Give me a second..."*
- **"Like..."** (as a filler, not a comparison) → Replace with nothing — just don't say it
- **"You know?"** → Not needed — drop it
- **"So, like, basically..."** → Replace with: *"Essentially..."* or *"In other words..."*
- **"I don't know"** (as a full answer) → Replace with: *"Let me think through this out loud..."*

### Better filler phrases (natural, professional, buy you time):
- *"Let me think through this for a moment..."* (pause)
- *"Let me reason through this out loud..."*
- *"Give me just a second to trace this..."*
- *"I want to make sure I get this right..."*
- *"Let me think about what I know here..."*
- *"One moment — I'm working through the logic..."*

**Key rule:** A 2-3 second pause with nothing said is completely acceptable. A 20-second silence is not. If you need more than 3 seconds, fill it with one of the phrases above.

## PART 7 — How to Sound Natural Despite an Accent

If English is your second language, these habits will make you sound clearer and more confident:

1. **Slow down, not just volume up.** Speaking slightly slower than feels natural to you sounds perfectly natural to a listener. Nervousness makes everyone speak faster.

2. **Pause after the key word.** *"I'll use Dijkstra's [pause] — because all the edge weights are positive [pause] — and it runs in O of V plus E log V."* Natural pauses between clauses are easier to understand than a continuous stream.

3. **Use shorter sentences.** Instead of one long compound sentence, use several shorter ones. *"The approach is Dijkstra's. All weights are positive, so BFS wouldn't work. Dijkstra's gives shortest path in O of V plus E log V."*

4. **It is okay to ask: "Did that make sense?"** at the end of a complex explanation. This is natural, not a weakness. It invites confirmation and shows you care about being understood.

5. **If you say a word wrong, correct it calmly.** *"The... memoization — sorry, meh-mo-eye-ZAY-shun — stores results of each subproblem."* One self-correction is totally fine.

6. **The interviewer is on your side.** They are not listening for accent. They are listening for reasoning. Your reasoning matters far more than your accent.
